What Is a HEIC File? A Plain-English Explainer

If you've ever tried to email a photo from your iPhone and gotten a confused message back — "I can't open this file" — there's a good chance the culprit is HEIC. It's not a typo, and it's not a broken file. HEIC is simply the format your iPhone has been using to save photos since 2017, and it behaves a little differently from the JPG files most people grew up with.

This post explains what HEIC actually is, why Apple chose it, and how to think about when it's fine to leave a photo as HEIC versus when you should convert it to something more universal like JPG or PNG.

HEIC, in one sentence

HEIC stands for High Efficiency Image Container, and it's Apple's implementation of the HEIF (High Efficiency Image Format) standard — a way of storing photos that squeezes out more detail per megabyte than the older JPEG format most cameras and software have used for decades.

Why Apple switched to it

Before iOS 11, iPhones saved photos as JPG, the same format that's been the default for digital cameras since the 1990s. JPG works well, but it's an old format built around compression techniques that haven't fundamentally changed in a long time. As camera sensors on phones got sharper and photo libraries grew larger, storage started to matter more.

HEIC uses a modern compression method that can store a photo at roughly half the file size of an equivalent-quality JPG. For a phone that might be holding tens of thousands of photos, that adds up fast — it's a big part of why an iPhone with the same storage capacity as an older model can suddenly hold noticeably more photos and videos.

What makes HEIC different, technically

Without getting too deep into the weeds, a few things set HEIC apart from JPG:

  • Better compression: HEIC uses a newer encoding method (based on HEVC, the same family of technology used in some video compression) that preserves more detail at a smaller file size.
  • Multiple images in one file: A single .heic file can technically hold more than one image — this is part of how Live Photos and burst shots are handled.
  • Support for transparency and higher color depth: HEIC can store more color information than a standard JPG, which matters for HDR photos.

So why doesn't everything open HEIC files?

HEIC is still a relatively newer format compared to JPG, and licensing around the underlying HEVC compression technology has made some companies slower to add support. The result is a patchwork: recent versions of Windows and macOS handle HEIC reasonably well, but plenty of websites, older software, work applications, and Android phones still expect a plain JPG or PNG and either can't open a HEIC file at all or display it as a broken image icon.

This is the single most common reason people end up needing to convert a HEIC photo — not because anything is wrong with the file, but because whatever they're trying to use it in hasn't caught up yet.

When you don't need to convert

If you're just looking at, editing, or sharing photos within Apple's own ecosystem — Photos app to Photos app, AirDrop between iPhones, iMessage — HEIC works fine and you'll never notice it's not a JPG. iOS even automatically converts HEIC to JPG in the background when you share a photo to an app or service that doesn't support HEIC, so a lot of the time this happens invisibly.

When you should convert

Conversion becomes worth doing when you run into one of these situations:

  • Uploading a photo to a website, job application, or online form that rejects the .heic extension
  • Sending a photo to someone using Windows or an Android phone who reports they can't open it
  • Using a photo in software that doesn't recognize HEIC — some older editing tools, presentation software, or web publishing platforms
  • Printing at a photo kiosk or lab that only accepts JPG

JPG or PNG — which one?

Once you've decided to convert, the next question is which format to convert to. As a rule of thumb: JPG is the safer default for everyday sharing, printing, and uploading, since it keeps file sizes small and is supported absolutely everywhere. PNG is worth choosing when you plan to edit the photo further, need a completely lossless copy for archiving, or you're working with design software where compression artifacts would be more noticeable. The bottom line

HEIC isn't a mistake or a problem with your phone — it's a genuinely more efficient way to store photos, and Apple's decision to switch to it as the default makes sense from a storage standpoint. The friction only shows up when a HEIC file meets software that hasn't added support for it, and in those moments, a quick conversion to JPG or PNG is the simplest fix. Once you know that's what's happening, it stops being confusing and just becomes a two-click task.
